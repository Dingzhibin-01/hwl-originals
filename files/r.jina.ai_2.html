The Royal Government of Bhutan has policies in place to attract foreign investment and has said it plans to liberalize its existing foreign direct investment (FDI) regime through the adoption of new regulations relating to labor, land, foreign currency, and repatriation of profits. The government has announced its objective to increase FDI into Bhutan to $5.9 billion (500 billion ngultrum) by 2029, and in 2024 the government approved 17 FDI projects worth over $183 million. Bhutan’s real GDP grew by 4.8 percent in fiscal year 2024-2025, and it is forecast to grow by 5.2 percent in fiscal year 2025-2026, ranking Bhutan among the fastest growing economies in South Asia (Bhutan’s fiscal year runs from July 1 to June 30). In February 2025, Bhutan introduced its “10X National Economic Vision,” which aims to increase GDP tenfold by 2050 by expanding existing growth sectors, such as hydropower, agriculture, and tourism, as well as emerging sectors, such as digital economy, services, manufacturing, and mining. Bhutan seeks to develop projects that would generate an estimated 15 gigawatts of hydropower electricity and 5 gigawatts of solar power electricity by 2040. Obstacles to Bhutan’s economic growth targets include its mountainous topography, dispersed population, deficit of skilled labor, and ongoing youth outmigration crisis. Bhutan’s Gelephu Mindfulness City (GMC), a multi-billion-dollar special autonomous region (SAR) project on the border with India, will seek investors globally. The initiative is in early stages of development and includes plans to construct an international airport, hydropower plant, and rail links with India. GMC will be administered separately from the rest of the country under a legal regime based on those of Singapore and the Abu Dhabi Global Market. Openness to, and Restrictions upon, Foreign Investment # Policies toward Foreign Direct Investment The government adopted Bhutan’s latest Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) policy in 2019 (amended in 2020), and it is currently considering revisions that could substantially liberalize the FDI regime. In 2024, the Ministry of Industry, Commerce, and Employment (MoICE) approved 17 FDI projects worth over $183 million. Since Bhutan introduced its first FDI policy in 2002, it has attracted 121 FDI projects valued at over $692 million. FDI is focused primarily in two sectors: hotels and resorts and information technology, and most businesses are clustered in the capital, Thimphu, or in Paro and Chhukha provinces. Other sectors with substantial FDI include hydropower, tourism, agriculture, manufacturing, and services. Invest Bhutan , which falls under MoICE and was established in 2016, is the government’s lead office for facilitating foreign investment. The Economic Development Board, headed by the Prime Minister, is responsible for streamlining the investment process to attract FDI. Under the FDI policy of 2019 , FDI companies are entitled to equal treatment and the same incentives as wholly domestically invested companies. However, FDI companies are required to facilitate the transfer of skills to Bhutanese nationals by employing five Bhutanese for every expatriate by the fifth year of operations. A review of Bhutan’s FDI policy conducted by MoICE with business and industry stakeholders found that restrictive travel and visa policies deterred investors from exploring opportunities in Bhutan, and the 49-percent maximum foreign equity permitted in most joint ventures discouraged some manufacturing firms from entering Bhutan’s market. Incentives for foreign investors include tax holidays in select sectors and exemptions on import duties and sales tax on machinery and raw materials. These incentives are also available to domestic investors, who sometimes receive preferential treatment in non-priority sectors. The Bhutan Chamber of Commerce and Industry (BCCI) is the apex body of the private sector in Bhutan and is active in consultation mechanisms with the government, representing the interests of domestic investors and joint ventures, including by organizing forums and dialogues Page 3 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State to convey investor concerns and suggest policy reforms. # Limits on Foreign Control and Right to Private Ownership and Establishment Bhutan permits 100 percent foreign ownership in certain sectors such as healthcare, luxury hotels and resorts, information technology, infrastructure (on a public private partnership model), and research and development. Bhutan allows 74 percent ownership in some sectors that include some agriculture activities, construction, consulting, education, finance, and manufacturing. There is a “negative list” of sectors in which foreign investment is only allowed through joint ventures, including media, broadcasting, distribution services, mining, 3-star and below hotels, health services, arms, and tobacco. The FDI policy requires a minimum project cost of $58,860 (5 million ngultrum) and limits foreign equity ownership in joint ventures to 49 percent. Minimum investment amounts and maximum foreign equity limits vary by sector, with project costs ranging from $58,860 (5 million ngultrum) for small-scale investments to $3.5 million (50 million ngultrum) for the education sector. Foreign investors can lease land for up to 30 years, with the option to renew the lease. FDI contributions must be made in convertible currency sourced directly from the foreign investor. FDI companies are required to retain 100 percent of invested foreign equity for three years from the start of commercial operations, and changes to the foreign investor are allowed only if there is no withdrawal of the invested foreign equity. The government permits repatriation of capital and dividends in the same currency of investment, and existing limitations on repatriation are expected to be liberalized in pending amendments to FDI legislation. Investment and Taxation Treaties Bhutan has free trade agreements with India and Thailand and a preferential trade agreement with Bangladesh. Bhutan and India have several taxation agreements, including a double taxation avoidance agreement and customs exemptions. Bhutan is a member of the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC) , together with India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Thailand. There is no tax treaty or totalization agreement between the United States and Bhutan. # Section 3. Legal Regime # Transparency of the Regulatory System The government and its policies generally foster market-based competition, although the Page 5 of 22 regulatory environment continues to evolve for foreign investors. MoICE plays a key role in regulating business activities, with national-level regulations being most relevant for foreign businesses. According to industry stakeholders, investment laws and policies lack clarity for foreign investors and implementation and enforcement can be inconsistent, often due to resource restraints. Publicly listed companies adhere to international financial reporting standards, but smaller businesses or those in the informal sector may have inconsistent accounting practices. While legal procedures may be clearly enumerated in relevant laws and regulations, enforcement can be slow and inefficient. Bhutan’s judiciary is independent, but access to timely legal recourse can be challenging due to the limited pool of legal professionals in the country. Investment regulations are developed through a consultative process involving multiple stakeholders, including government ministries and agencies and the private sector. Draft bills and regulations are made available for public comment. However, the process is not always formalized or widely publicized. Investors and business associations such as BCCI often provide input during consultations on investment regulations. Regulations and updates are posted on the e-Regulations Bhutan and Integrated Business Licensing Service portals and/ or the websites of relevant ministries and agencies. Regulatory changes may also be communicated through official press releases or public announcements in local media. The Royal Audit Authority audits government expenditures and ensures compliance with the law. The National Assembly , one of two chambers of Bhutan’s bicameral legislature, promotes government accountability through debates, discussions, and committee reviews. Civil society organizations, NGOs, and the press also play a role in holding the government accountable, especially for decisions affecting social or environmental outcomes. Various government agencies have regulatory enforcement responsibilities. MoICE enforces laws related to business, investment, and trade. The Ministry of Finance (MoF) oversees tax Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State Page 6 of 22 laws and government expenditure. The National Environment Commission is mandated to protect, conserve, and improve the natural environment. The Royal Civil Service Commission ensures public servants adhere to ethical and legal standards. The Judiciary reviews regulatory enforcement actions and settles disputes. # International Regulatory Considerations Bhutan actively participates in regional organizations like the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) and BIMSTEC . Bhutan’s trade policy and foreign investment regulations often align with those of India due to the close economic and political ties between the two countries. Publicly listed companies and large businesses in Bhutan follow international best practices for accounting and financial reporting. Bhutan is not a member of the WTO. # Legal System and Judicial Independence Bhutan’s legal system integrates elements of civil and common law, as well as some theocratic elements drawing from traditional Buddhist principles, particularly in matters of personal and social justice. The country has a modern statutory legal system influenced by Indian and British practices. Contracts in Bhutan are legally enforceable and governed by the Contract Act of Bhutan 2013 ,with disputes handled by the courts. The legal framework is guided by the Civil and Criminal Procedure Code of Bhutan. Key commercial laws include the Companies Act of Bhutan of 2016 ,tax laws, and intellectual property laws. Bhutan’s judiciary is independent. The Constitution grants the King of Bhutan the authority to appoint the judiciary. Efforts to ensure judicial impartiality include the Judiciary Service Act of Bhutan . The judicial process is generally fair but faces complaints regarding efficiency, accessibility, and transparency. Initial appeals typically go through lower courts or designated administrative bodies. # Laws and Regulations on Foreign Direct Investment Bhutan’s legal system provides protections for foreign investors. The key frameworks for FDI are laid out in the Constitution, the Companies Act of Bhutan of 2016 , the RMA’s Foreign Exchange Regulations , and the FDI Policy of 2019 . While judicial decisions related to foreign investment are not commonly published in Bhutan, such disputes are typically resolved in accordance with Bhutanese civil and commercial law. InvestBhutan.gov.bt serves as the primary source of information on investment opportunities, laws, rules, procedures, and reporting requirements in Bhutan. It provides guidance on setting up a business, obtaining licenses, and navigating the regulatory environment for both local and foreign investors and includes information on available incentives, tax policies, and sector-specific guidelines. The Government of Bhutan provides a centralized platform for foreign investors through the eRegulations page. # Competition and Antitrust Laws Bhutan’s Constitution has provisions that aim to prevent commercial monopolies. The Competition and Consumer Affairs Authority under MoICE has a mandate to prevent anticompetitive, deceptive, and unfair business practices, including through law enforcement. # Expropriation and Compensation The Land Act of Bhutan 2007 allows the government to acquire land for national development, infrastructure projects, or other public purposes, with compensation provided to affected property owners, usually at market value. The law outlines processes for resolving disputes and specifies that compensation should be fair. Some major projects, such as the construction of hydropower plants, have required large land acquisitions, affecting rural farmers and residents and leading to reports of community Page 8 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State dissatisfaction due to displacement, insufficient compensation, or delays. In urban areas like Thimphu, land expropriation for expansion has also spurred complaints about inadequate compensation and slow processes. There have been allegations of government actions that diminish the economic value of investments, especially in hydropower, real estate, and natural resources, including concerns over regulatory changes, payment delays, and environmental restrictions. Tax policies can be perceived as unpredictable, affecting investment returns. # Dispute Settlement ICSID Convention and New York Convention Bhutan is not a signatory to the Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States (ICSID). Bhutan has been a party to the New York Convention of 1958 on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards since 2014 and courts are obliged to enforce settlements under the Convention. Bhutan has a domestic arbitration system governed by the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act of Bhutan 2013 , which provides for arbitration as a means of resolving disputes within the country. Investor-State Dispute Settlement Bhutan has trade agreements with India, Bangladesh, and Thailand. These agreements generally include provisions for international arbitration under frameworks like UNCITRAL or ICSID, depending on the specific terms. There have been no reported high-profile investment disputes involving U.S. or other foreign investors under international arbitration in the past decade. If a foreign arbitral award is issued against the Bhutanese government, it would require a recognition process through Bhutan’s domestic courts. There have been no publicly reported cases where Bhutan has faced damages in investor-state disputes from international arbitration. Bhutan’s limited engagement in international Page 9 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State arbitration and cautious approach to foreign investment make it difficult to assess the government’s track record of paying damages. There is no history of extrajudicial actions against foreign investors in Bhutan. International Commercial Arbitration and Foreign Courts In Bhutan, alternative dispute resolution methods like mediation, conciliation, and arbitration are recognized as effective ways to resolve disputes outside of formal court proceedings. Bhutan’s domestic arbitration system is governed by the Bhutan Alternative Dispute Resolution Act of 2013, which provides the legal framework for arbitration within the country. This law facilitates both domestic and international arbitration and establishes rules for conducting arbitration proceedings, including foreign-issued arbitral awards. Bhutan allows for the recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards, but to seek enforcement, the foreign investor may need to file a recognition petition in a Bhutanese court. # Bankruptcy/Insolvency Regulations The Bankruptcy Act of Bhutan 1999 provides mechanisms for dealing with financially distressed companies, including how creditors, equity shareholders, and holders of other financial contracts are treated in the event of insolvency. The Bankruptcy Act covers both corporate and individual insolvency. Bhutan’s bankruptcy law is generally based on principles of liquidation and reorganization, and creditors have the right to be consulted during the disposition process. # Section 4. Industrial Policies # Investment Incentives Bhutan offers a variety of incentives aimed at attracting foreign investors that, depending on the sector, may include corporate tax benefits; tax credits and exemptions for priority sectors; reduced cost of land, particularly for large scale investments and in industrial parks; and customs and import duty exemptions. Page 10 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State The government supports public-private partnerships (PPPs) and infrastructure development by providing land, regulatory facilitation, and financial incentives. In priority sectors like agriculture, manufacturing, and tourism, the government offers benefits such as tax breaks, land leases, and subsidized financing. For strategic investments in energy or infrastructure, incentives are applied on a case-by-case basis, with terms negotiated based on the project’s alignment with Bhutan’s development goals. MoICE operates five industrial parks that cater to foreign investors. # Foreign Trade Zones/Free Ports/Trade Facilitation The Gelephu Mindfulness City (GMC) Special Autonomous Region (SAR), introduced by King Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck in December 2023, is a developing initiative that aims to attract foreign investment and boost Bhutan’s regional connectivity. The GMC SAR is situated on an area of more than 2,500 square kilometers in the southern plains of Bhutan, bordering India’s Assam state. The SAR is administered by the Gelephu Mindfulness City Authority (GMCA), which is independent of the Royal Government of Bhutan. Still in its infancy, the initiative is slated to include construction of an international airport with operations projected to begin in 2029, a new hydropower plant, and eventually accommodate an international population of over one million. The governments of Bhutan and India are planning to construct a rail link between GMC and Assam, India, that would link the SAR with transport corridors to Bangladesh and Southeast Asia. According to promotional materials, the aim is for GMC to become a high-tech hub for businesses across Asia focused on seven core sectors: agri-tech and forestry, aviation and logistics, education and knowledge, finance and digital assets, green energy and technology, health and wellness, and spirituality. In December 2024, the GMC Authority announced the enactment of GMC Law No. 1 of 2024 , which provides the governing framework for the GMC SAR. The law integrates Singapore’s common law and Abu Dhabi Global Market regulations to govern company incorporation, employment, taxation, and financial services in GMC. GMC is led by a board of directors chaired by the King. In agriculture, eco-tourism, and manufacturing, the government promotes using local raw materials, machinery, and labor to support local businesses and create jobs. For instance, eco-tourism projects are encouraged to hire local tour guides and suppliers to benefit the community. Sector-specific performance reports may be required, where foreign investors must update on their use of local content and employment of local workers. Performance requirements apply uniformly to both domestic and foreign investors, varying by sector and project type. There is no formal requirement for foreign IT providers to turn over source code or provide access to encryption, but firms in sensitive sectors could face data protection regulations. The National Digital Identity Act of Bhutan 2023 regulates the transmission of data outside Bhutan and requires that international data transfers are protected adequately. # Section 5. Protection of Property Rights # Real Property Property rights are legally recognized and enforced in Bhutan. The Land Act of Bhutan 2007 is the principal legislation governing land matters, covering private, government, community, and institutional land ownership. Property rights are registered with the National Land Commission, which maintains land records and oversees land administration. Mortgages can be registered against land, and they are commonly used as collateral for loans. Liens are also recognized and must be registered. The National Cadastral Information System is the digital system used to maintain these records. Page 12 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State Foreigners and non-resident investors can lease land for economic development purposes, such as hydropower, tourism, or agriculture projects. These leases must be approved by the National Land Commission and relevant government ministries. Key regulatory references include the Land Act, the FDI policy, and the Rules and Regulations for Lease of Government Reserved Forest Land 2009. A sizeable portion of land in Bhutan may not have clearly defined ownership due to the lack of a comprehensive land registration system and complexities of traditional use rights and land disputes. The National Cadastral Resurvey Program was launched in 2008 to address this, and by 2016, over 90 percent of privately held land had been re-surveyed and titles updated. However, unresolved cases remain, especially in rural areas, near government or forest land, and urban expansions with informal settlements. The Land Act strengthened the National Land Commission’s authority to resolve disputes, cancel illegal encroachments, and streamline land transfer and registration procedures. Once property is legally purchased and registered, ownership is protected by law. The Land Act recognizes registered owners and outlines the process for transferring, leasing, and inheriting property. The law does not allow property to be reverted to squatters or other individuals due to lack of occupation. Property ownership is protected if it is legally registered in the owner’s name. Bhutan is a contracting party to the 2001 Cape Town Convention on Mobile Equipment and the Protocol on Matters Specific to Aircraft Equipment, which came into force in 2006. There is no specific record of disputes under the Cape Town Convention and Aircraft Protocol involving Bhutan. # Intellectual Property Legislation for intellectual property (IP) protection in Bhutan include the Industrial Property Act of 2001 and the Copyright Act of 2001 , along with subsequent regulations and amendments. Bhutan is a member of the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) and has signed Page 13 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State international treaties such as the Paris Convention and the Berne Convention. Enforcement of IP rights in Bhutan faces challenges due to limited infrastructure, legal awareness, and resources. The Department of Media, Creative Industry, and Intellectual Property under MoICE is responsible for IP registration and enforcement, but public awareness and capacity are still developing. IP infringement cases are relatively rare, partly due to limited commercial activity involving high-value IP. However, a counterfeit goods market exists, particularly for electronics, clothing, and luxury items. Bhutan does not have a specific law for trade secrets, but they can be protected through contracts such as non-disclosure agreements. There have been no major publicized IP violations prosecuted in Bhutan’s courts over the past decade. As of December 2024, there were no new laws or pending IP reform bills specifically addressing AI or related technological developments in Bhutan. Customs authorities have confiscated counterfeit goods, especially those smuggled through borders. However, there are no widely published official figures or detailed statistics on the seizure of counterfeit goods, and Bhutan does not have a dedicated public-facing registry for reporting counterfeit seizures. Bhutan is not listed in the U.S. Trade Representative’s (USTR) Special 301 Report or the Notorious Markets Report. For additional information about national laws and points of contact at local IP offices, please see WIPO’s country profiles . # Section 6. Financial Sector # Capital Markets and Portfolio Investment The Royal Securities Exchange of Bhutan (RSEB), established in 1993, had a market capitalization of around $738 million and 19 listed companies as of December 2024. The RSEB is a member of the South Asian Federation of Exchanges. It was established under the RMA and incorporated under the Companies Act of the Kingdom of Bhutan 2016. The RSEB is Page 14 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State regulated by the Financial Services Act 2011. The Asian Development Bank reported in 2021 that Bhutan’s government bond market was underdeveloped, with an issued debt-to-GDP ratio of 5.8 percent of estimated 2020 GDP and no credit rating agency or regulatory framework for bond issuance. Bhutan lacks regulations for underwriting equity or debt issues. Bhutan maintains three exchange restrictions requiring approval under IMF Article VIII, Section 2(a). These restrictions include the following requirements: • FDI projects importing capital goods and certain industrial projects importing primary raw materials must meet foreign exchange balancing requirements; • FDI companies must cover operational expenses, including insurance, using their own foreign exchange resources; and • Bhutanese companies must pay interest and amortize external loans using their own foreign currency resources. Bhutan’s capital markets are closed to foreign investors, contributing to low trading volumes and slow growth. Access to finance remains a significant challenge, especially for smaller and new businesses, despite increasing credit to the private sector. Government initiatives such as the 2024 Economic Stimulus Plan and the 2020 National Credit Guarantee Scheme aim to improve access to finance. Under this scheme, cottage and small businesses, including startups, are eligible for 100 percent debt financing, and medium and large businesses can also obtain loans. However, the requirement for collateral to secure loans remains an obstacle for aspiring entrepreneurs. # Money and Banking System The RMA serves as both an autonomous central bank and the regulator of financial service providers in Bhutan. The country has five commercial banks, three insurance companies, five microfinance institutions, and one pension institution. Commercial banks include Bank of Bhutan, Bhutan Development Bank, Bhutan National Bank, Druk Punjab National Bank, and Page 15 of 22 Tashi Bank. The RMA reported in 2022 that 76 percent of Bhutanese had savings accounts, and 22 percent had access to loans. As of 2020, 95 percent of people living in urban areas had bank accounts, compared to 67 percent of rural inhabitants. About 40 percent of households held some form of debt. The financial health of Bhutan’s financial sector is evaluated using the Capital Adequacy, Asset Quality, Management, Earnings, and Liquidity approach, which intends to ensure that institutions can absorb losses, maintain liquidity, and sustain profitability. According to the RMA’s 2023 Annual Supervision Report , the capital adequacy ratio improved from 14.22 percent in 2019 to 16.87 percent in 2023, surpassing the minimum requirement of 12.5 percent, indicating robust capital accumulation and improved risk management. As of December 2023, the combined assets of Bhutan’s financial institutions were close to $3.5 billion, with term loans making up 53.52 percent of total assets. Bhutan allows FDI in the banking sector, with up to 51 percent foreign ownership permitted in financial services, subject to the same regulations as local banks. Foreign investment in the banking sector requires clearance from MoICE and the RMA. Foreigners with a valid special residence permit or work permit and a registered mobile number are permitted to open bank accounts in Bhutan. # Foreign Exchange and Remittances Foreign Exchange Bhutan permits FDI businesses to convert earnings into foreign currencies, but prior approval from the RMA is required for repatriation of dividends and other transactions. FDI companies must arrange foreign exchange capital from their own sources for investment. They can purchase convertible currency from the RMA for importing raw materials, purchasing capital goods, paying interest and amortization, royalties, and remittances of remuneration. FDI companies must maintain a foreign currency account with a local bank, and transactions must be routed through these banks. Investors are allowed to raise loans and borrow from financial institutions within the country. Dividend repatriation accumulated over the preceding three years will be in the currency of earnings. Investors can purchase convertible currency for repatriation of dividends up to $5 million per annum for investments in the service sector, where the investment was in convertible currency and earnings are in non-convertible currencies. The Bhutanese ngultrum (BTN) has been pegged to the Indian rupee (INR) at par since 1974. The ngultrum does not have an independent exchange rate against major currencies like the U.S. dollar or euro; its exchange rate against these currencies is determined by the INR’s exchange rate and can fluctuate accordingly. Remittance Policies The Foreign Exchange Rules and Regulations 2022 , last updated in 2024, cover the management of foreign exchange. The Inward Remittance Rules and Regulations 2023 outline requirements and procedures for authorized banks and money transfer agents involved in processing inward remittances by Bhutanese living abroad. Bhutan encourages inward remittances and the RMA’s RemitBhutan Platform facilitates non-resident Bhutanese in opening foreign currency accounts in Bhutan. The RMA has at times offered temporary incentives for remitting funds to Bhutan. While new regulations grant the right to repatriate dividends without restrictions, they do not specify a standard timeframe for processing these outward remittances. # Sovereign Wealth Funds Druk Holding and Investments Limited (DHI) is the commercial and investment arm of the Bhutanese government. State-owned Enterprises Bhutan has 30 state-owned enterprises (SOE), of which 29 are public limited companies; the National Pension and Provident Fund is an autonomous body. In 2021, Bhutan’s SOE portfolio had total assets of $3.71 billion (319.1 billion ngultrum), equivalent to 207 percent of GDP. The SOEs generated revenues of $663.9 million (56.9 billion ngultrum), accounting for 30 percent of GDP, and reported a net profit of $187.85 million (16.1 billion ngultrum). The Ministry of Finance fully owns 14 companies and holds a 51 percent stake in one, while DHI fully owns 10 entities and is a majority stakeholder in five. SOEs employed 11,194 people as of 2021. SOEs under the Ministry of Finance operate in sectors such as manufacturing, trading, services, and financial institutions. SOEs under DHI have stakes in energy and resources, communication and transport, finance, real estate and construction, trading, and manufacturing. In 2004, the government issued guidelines for SOE boards, including standard rules for corporate governance and oversight. The government manages state ownership through a dual model in which the Ministry of Finance and a designated administrative ministry jointly exercise ownership rights over each SOE. External auditors and the Royal Audit Authority conduct reviews of these enterprises. The boards of the SOEs, Parliament, the Cabinet of Ministers, and sector-specific regulators all provide oversight. Many Bhutanese SOEs operate in an environment with limited competition. The government holds minority stakes in Bhutan Board Products Ltd, Bhutan Ferro Alloys Ltd, Bhutan National Bank Ltd, Penden Cement Authority Ltd, and Royal Insurance Corporation of Bhutan. A list of SOEs in Bhutan can be found in the Ministry of Finance’s SOE Annual Report , which was last published in 2021. Bhutan’s legal and regulatory framework for SOEs is largely compatible with the OECD Principles of Corporate Governance. # Privatization Program Bhutan initiated privatization programs in the 1980s, particularly in the tourism sector, and the government has identified the private sector as a key engine for economic growth and employment, promoting such initiatives as the Public Private Partnership Policy of 2016 . International financial institutions such as the World Bank, Asian Development Bank, and the International Finance Corporation have invested in Bhutan, supporting private sector development in key sectors like tourism, agriculture, financial services, and cottage and small industries. Bhutan has a public bidding process for privatized sectors, particularly for public private partnerships (PPPs), with the Ministry of Finance playing a role in managing and implementing these projects. PPP projects require consultation with the private sector, including project developers, contractors, and sources of finance, to ensure projects are taken up by the market and tendered competitively. The Public Private Partnership Policy from 2016 , Public Private Partnership Rules and Regulations 2017 , and PPP Guidelines 2019 provide additional details. # Section 8. Corruption Bhutan has a legal and institutional framework in place to combat corruption. The Anti- Corruption Commission (ACC) was established in 2005 and serves as an independent constitutional body tasked with preventing and investigating corruption in both public and private sectors. The Anti-Corruption Act of Bhutan 2011 , amended in 2022 , criminalizes bribery, abuse of functions, conflict of interest, and illicit enrichment. It applies to public officials, their family members, and, in some contexts, political parties. The Civil and Criminal Procedure Codes and Penal Code of Bhutan also support anti-corruption efforts. The ACC is seen as active and relatively effective, regularly investigating and prosecuting Page 19 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State corruption cases, although capacity limitations and resource constraints may affect its overall reach. Laws are generally applied in a non-discriminatory manner, although there have been concerns about selective enforcement in some high-profile cases. Bhutan has explicit conflict-of-interest provisions, particularly in relation to government procurement and public contracts. The Public Procurement Rules and Regulations provide transparency guidelines, requiring competitive bidding and public disclosure of awarded contracts. Government employees must declare their assets and liabilities annually. While the government encourages companies to adopt codes of conduct prohibiting bribery, there is no legal requirement for private firms to do so. Uptake of corporate compliance programs is limited, especially among small and medium enterprises. Bhutan signed the United Nations Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC) in September 2003 and has made commitments under its framework. The ACC collaborates with regional partners under SAARC’s anti-corruption mechanisms and participates in international training and information-sharing platforms. Civil society organizations and media play a limited role in exposing corruption. The civil society organization Bhutan Transparency Initiative advocates for increased transparency, integrity, and accountability to counter corruption. Non-government actors involved in anti-corruption efforts are not overtly persecuted but may face constraints due to self-censorship. There is limited legal protection for whistleblowers and non-government investigators. Political and Security Environment Politically motivated violence or civil disturbance is uncommon. Bhutan ranks high in political stability in global indices. Bhutan transitioned peacefully into a constitutional monarchy in 2008. National Assembly elections in January 2024 were widely reported to be fair and free of abuses and irregularities. The U.S. State Department ranks Bhutan at Level 1 at travel.state.gov , advising U.S. travelers to exercise normal precautions in Bhutan. Minor grievances have occasionally emerged related to local governance or policies. A few localized environmental concerns have been raised over new projects, such as road or dam development, but these have generally been resolved through dialogue. # Section 10. Labor Policies and Practices According to Bhutan’s 2024 Labor Force Survey report , the overall unemployment rate in Bhutan decreased from 5.9 percent in 2022 to 3.5 percent in 2023. Youth unemployment dropped from 28.6 percent to 15.9 percent during the same period. The World Bank estimates that Bhutan’s informal economy constitutes approximately 23.8 percent of its GDP. In 2022, self-employed individuals accounted for about 71 percent of total employment, reflecting the dominance of informal labor arrangements, particularly in sectors like agriculture, street vending, and home-based enterprises. Bhutan faces notable shortages in specialized labor across several key sectors, including construction, tourism, production, agriculture, creative industries, and digital technologies. The government is attempting to address the skills gap by increasing technical and vocational Page 21 of 22 Investment Climate Statement for Bhutan | 2025 U.S. Department of State education and training for roughly 50 percent of the workforce by 2029, increasing to 80 percent by 2033. The government’s De-suung Skilling Programme offers short-term training in various fields for Bhutanese youth entering the job market. The 2019 FDI policy mandates that FDI companies train and employ Bhutanese nationals at all levels, and mandates a ratio of five Bhutanese employees for every expatriate by the fifth year of operations. Revised FDI rules that are currently under consideration will reportedly eliminate or modify these requirements and allow FDI companies greater flexibility in hiring expatriate professionals. Collective bargaining is not common, and its coverage across the economy is minimal. The legal framework allows for labor unions and collective bargaining, but union activity is extremely limited. The Labour and Employment Act of 2007 provides a legal framework to regulate employment practices, covering aspects such as employment contracts, working conditions, wage disputes, and dispute resolution. The Department of Labour, which falls under MoICE, oversees labor relations, including dispute resolution. Unresolved disputes are adjudicated by an arbitrator or a court. Detailed information on dispute resolution is included in the Regulations on Working Conditions of 2022. Compliance with labor regulations in Bhutan varies by business size. Only about 30 percent of small firms adhere to occupational health and safety standards, and just 12 percent have sexual harassment policies, compared to over 90 percent compliance in larger enterprises. This discrepancy is likely due to economic constraints faced by smaller firms. The prominence of the informal sector can influence market dynamics and industry access. Informal businesses often operate with lower overhead costs, potentially creating uneven competition for formal enterprises. Foreign companies may encounter challenges in sectors where informal operations are widespread, as these businesses might not adhere to standard regulatory and quality norms, complicating market entry and fair competition. Bhutan is a member of the International Labor Organization and generally in good standing with its obligations.
